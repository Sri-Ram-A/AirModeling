Haversine Distance

The Earth is a sphere. Between two stations we need the great-circle distance — not flat Euclidean geometry. The Haversine formula gives us this accurately.

Step-by-Step Derivation
1
Convert coordinates to radians
φ₁ = lat₁ × π/180, φ₂ = lat₂ × π/180 Δφ = (lat₂ − lat₁) × π/180 Δλ = (lon₂ − lon₁) × π/180
All trigonometric functions work in radians, so we convert degrees first.
2
Compute the Haversine intermediate term a
$$a = \sin^2\!\!\left(\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2}\right) + \cos(\varphi_1)\cdot\cos(\varphi_2)\cdot\sin^2\!\!\left(\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2}\right)$$
This is the half-versed-sine of the central angle. a ∈ [0,1] — it's always non-negative.
3
Convert to distance using arcsin
$$d = 2R \cdot \arcsin\!\!\left(\sqrt{a}\right), \quad R = 6{,}371 \text{ km}$$
R is Earth's mean radius. arcsin(√a) gives half the central angle; multiplying by 2R converts to arc length.
4
Convert km → metres for plume model
d_m = d_km × 1000
The Pasquill-Gifford dispersion formula uses metres. Distances < 10 m are floored to 100 m (same-station self-emission).
Why Not Flat Geometry?

At distances of 1–50 km, the difference between Euclidean and great-circle distance is small (~0.01%) but at larger distances the Earth's curvature matters significantly. The Haversine formula is exact for a sphere.

Key insight:
The Haversine formula is numerically stable for both small distances (where sin≈θ) and large distances (antipodal points). It's the standard formula for all aviation and GIS applications.
